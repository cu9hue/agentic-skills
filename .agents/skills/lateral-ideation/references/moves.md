# Moves

Each move takes a seed and forces it somewhere a plain list would not go.
Draw them at random (see SKILL.md). Each entry gives the operation and one
example on a seed unrelated to agents, so the example does not anchor the
idea.

SCAMPER maps onto this list: S = Substitute, C = Combine, A = Adapt from
elsewhere, M = Maximize / Minimize, P = Put to another use, E = Eliminate,
R = Invert. When the user names SCAMPER, run those seven in order.

### Invert

Flip the direction of the main relationship: who acts and who is acted on,
what comes first and what comes last, who pays and who is paid. Source: de
Bono's reversal; SCAMPER's R. Example (a library): readers lend books to the
library, which pays them per borrow.

### Split

List the seed's parts or attributes, pick one, and change only that part as
far as it will go. Source: Michalko's attribute listing. Example (an
umbrella): handle, canopy, ribs, ownership. Change only ownership: umbrellas
belong to the street corner, not the person.

### Maximize

Multiply one quantity by 1,000: count, speed, size, frequency, or audience.
Ask what breaks and what becomes possible. Source: SCAMPER's M (magnify).
Example (a birthday party): 1,000 guests every day means the party is a
public venue with a host rota.

### Minimize

Divide one quantity by 1,000, or make the resource scarce and expensive. Ask
how people cope. Source: SCAMPER's M (minify). Example (email): ten emails a
year per person, so each one is an event and gets a printed reply.

### Substitute

Replace one component, material, person or step with something from a
different category. Source: SCAMPER's S. Example (a wedding): replace the
officiant with the couple's grandchildren, read from a script they wrote at
age eight.

### Combine

Fuse the seed with something unrelated that serves a different job, so each
changes the other. Source: SCAMPER's C. Example (a laundromat): combined with
a public library, the wash cycle becomes the loan period.

### Adapt from elsewhere

Find a field that already solved a similar problem and borrow its mechanism
whole. Source: SCAMPER's A. Example (hospital triage): adapted from airline
boarding groups, patients get a printed group letter at the door.

### Put to another use

Keep the thing as it is and give it a different job or a different user.
Source: SCAMPER's P. Example (a dashcam): used by a cyclist's insurer as a
road-quality survey, paid per kilometre filmed.

### Eliminate

Remove the part everyone treats as essential and see what remains. Source:
SCAMPER's E. Example (a restaurant): no menu, so the kitchen cooks what the
last table left uneaten, reworked.

### Change the actor

Hand the seed to a different person: the user's child, their parent, their
landlord, a regulator, a competitor, a criminal, someone a hundred years ago.
Ask what they would do with it. Example (a step counter): owned by the
landlord, it sets the rent discount for tenants who take the stairs.

### Po: escape

Name something the seed takes for granted, write "po" before its opposite,
and use that statement as a stepping stone, not a proposal. Source: de
Bono's po provocation. Example (a taxi): po, the taxi does not know where it
is going. Stepping stone: the passenger and driver negotiate the route as a
game, and the fare drops with each detour the passenger accepts.

### Po: wishful thinking

State an impossible wish about the seed as if it were true, then find the
nearest thing that could be true. Source: de Bono. Example (moving house):
po, the house moves with you. Nearest true thing: furniture rented from a
national pool, so you move only boxes.

### Challenge

Ask "why is it done this way?" of one ordinary feature, three times, until
the answer is "habit". Then design without that habit. Source: de Bono's
challenge. Example (school terms): long summer breaks follow a farm calendar
that most families no longer keep.

### Brutethink

Draw a random concept from the list below. List three of its properties.
Force each property onto the seed. Source: Michalko's Brutethink; de Bono's
random entry. Example (a gym, concept "harvest"): seasons, ripeness, a
shared crop. The gym runs in seasons, with a harvest week where members carry
each other's goals.

### Idea box

Pick three or four attributes of the seed and list four options for each in
a grid. Combine one option from each column at random, then make the
strangest combination work. Source: Michalko's idea box (after Zwicky's
morphological analysis). Example (a lunch box): material × owner × refill ×
schedule gives "ceramic, shared by a street, refilled by the café, weekly".

### Fail first

Describe the most likely way the seed fails in public: the bug, the lawsuit,
the headline, the angry review. Turn the failure into the product, or into
the defence against it. Example (smart locks): the lock bricks during a
heatwave, so the product is a mechanical key held by a neighbour and logged.

### Time shift

Place the seed 50 years in the past or 20 years in the future. Ask what it
would be made of, who would own it, and what would be illegal. Example (a
podcast in 1950): a record pressed weekly and mailed to subscribers, with a
reply postcard.

### Card

Draw one card from `cards.md` and apply it literally to the seed.
