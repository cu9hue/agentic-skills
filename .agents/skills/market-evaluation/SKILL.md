# Market Evaluation

When a great team meets a lousy market, the market wins. This skill judges
the market, not the team or the product. The bar is a company that can make
**$1bn+ in annual revenue in under 10 years**. A market that cannot carry
that is the wrong market for this ambition, however good the business is.

The evaluation asks nine questions, grouped into three tests:

- **Scale** — can this become enormous?
- **Truth** — is the problem proven and defensible?
- **Speed** — can you learn before you run out of time?

Every question gets a sourced bull case, a sourced bear case, and an integer
score from -5 to +5. A strong market passes all three tests. Never add the
scores up or average them: a sum lets one strong axis hide a fatal one.

## Pin the market first

Name the market in one line: the customer, the buyer (if different), the
geography, and the product shape. If the user left any of these out, pick
the most likely reading, state it as an assumption at the top, and run the
full evaluation. Do not stop to ask clarifying questions first. Offer at the
end to re-run on a different reading.

## Research rules

- Research before you score. Search for each axis. Put as much effort into
  the bear case as the bull case: look for shutdowns, down rounds, incumbent
  feature launches, price cuts, and failed predecessors.
- Every factual claim carries an inline source link. A claim with no source
  is labeled *(assumption)* or *(inference)*. Never present an estimate as a
  fact.
- Link the page that states the number. Before you cite a page, confirm
  that the page itself contains the figure — not a related page from the
  same publisher, and not a page you remember saying it. A number you
  computed from several sources is labeled *(inference)* and links each
  input. Load-bearing numbers get this check first, because the scores
  rest on them.
- Rank sources. Primary sources are strong: government statistics, company
  filings and earnings calls, and industry-association data. Vendor blogs
  and paid market-research press releases are weak. Say so when a claim
  rests on one.
- Give the year of every number. Flag data older than three years.

## The nine axes

### Scale test

**1. $1bn ARR path.** Can a company in this market reach $1bn annual
revenue in under 10 years? Build it bottom-up: (number of customers) ×
(revenue per customer). Source the customer count, for example from census
or association data. Source the price from comparable products. Show the
arithmetic. Then name the revenue shape. Janz's five ways to $100M ARR,
scaled ×10 for $1bn:

| Shape | Customers for $1bn | Revenue per customer / year |
|---|---|---|
| Elephants | 10,000 | $100k |
| Deer | 100,000 | $10k |
| Rabbits | 1M | $1k |
| Mice | 10M | $100 |
| Flies | 100M active users | $10 (ads) |

Bull: this market alone holds enough customers at a defensible price.
Bear: the math reaches $1bn only with heroic share (over ~30%), an unproven
price, or adjacencies nobody has named.

**2. US first.** If the market is not the US, why not? When a US and a
non-US version of the same idea exist, usually only one wins, and it is
usually the US one. For a non-US market, name the US equivalent market and
check whether a US player already exists. Score bearish unless there is a
specific, sourced reason that the smaller market wins. Examples: regulation
that does not transfer, a local incumbent structure that has no US
counterpart, or a problem that does not exist in the US. For a US market,
score how well the US is served as the launch market.

**3. Adjacencies.** What will the company own in year 3 that makes the next
product inevitable? Name the asset: data, workflow position, distribution,
trust, or installed base. Name the next one or two products. Bull: the
year-3 asset leads straight to them. Bear: the expansion is opportunistic,
the asset does not transfer, or an incumbent already owns the adjacency.

### Truth test

**4. Market dynamics.** Classify the market as crowded, untapped, or both.

- **Crowded** proves the demand. It also demands a wedge. Name the wedge,
  and name what the company will have in year 3 that a well-funded
  competitor starting today cannot copy. "Better product" is not an answer.
  List the funded competitors and incumbents with sources.
- **Untapped** demands an explanation. If this gap exists, why has nobody
  filled it? Name the reason with a source: the technology only just became
  possible, a regulation changed, or a cost curve crossed a threshold. No
  reason is a bear signal. The likeliest explanation is that the demand is
  not there.

**5. Problem size.** Does the problem carry economic weight? Put a dollar
figure on the money spent or lost on this problem across the market. Large
outcomes need a problem that is expensive at market scale.

**6. Pain depth.** Is the problem felt deeply enough? Name the budget line
item, or the shadow spend, that the product replaces. Shadow spend includes
headcount, agencies, spreadsheets, and manual workarounds. Deep pain shows
up as urgency, a budget, and repeat behavior. Bear: a nice-to-have with no
line item, or discretionary spend that gets cut first.

**7. Repeatability.** Does one customer teach you how to win the next? Look
for the same buyer, the same workflow, and the same integration from
customer to customer. Bear: every deal is custom, each customer needs its
own integration, or the segment is fragmented.

**8. Tailwinds.** Will the market be bigger in the future without the
company's help? Name two or three structural forces with sources:
regulation, demographics, cost curves, or platform shifts. Bear: the
tailwinds help incumbents as much as entrants, or a headwind is visible.

### Speed test

**9. Learning speed.** Can the company fail fast enough? Every market comes
with a go-to-market attached, and the go-to-market sets how many learning
cycles fit in 18 months. Name the buyer and the user, and source the
typical sales cycle.

| Fast loop — about 30 cycles in 18 months | Slow loop — 1 to 2 cycles in 18 months |
|---|---|
| A user can try it without asking anyone | The buyer is not the user; procurement gates entry |
| The value is obvious in hours | The value is obvious in quarters |
| Retention shows in week one | Each customer costs as much as the last |

Slow-loop markets build great companies, but they are hard to start in
without a network. If the founders' network would change the score, say
what it would take, and do not assume that they have one.

## Scoring

Write the bull case and the bear case first, then the score. The score is
one integer from -5 to +5, plus a confidence level (high, medium, or low)
that reflects source quality.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| +5 | Overwhelming sourced evidence for; the bear case is speculative |
| +3 | The bull case clearly outweighs; the bear case is real but has a named mitigation |
| +1 | Leans bullish; the evidence is mixed |
| 0 | Evenly balanced |
| -1 | Leans bearish; the evidence is mixed |
| -3 | The bear case clearly outweighs; the risk has no named mitigation |
| -5 | Disqualifying; the bull case is speculative |

Use ±2 and ±4 between those anchors. Thin evidence lowers the confidence,
not the score: never use 0 to mean "I don't know". Each score gets one
sentence that says which case outweighs the other and why.

Score the evidence, not the user's hopes. When the user needs the result to
look strong, for a pitch or a check-in, the weakest axis is exactly where
the audience will probe. Say plainly when a test fails, and give the
strongest honest version of the market in its place.

## Rollup: a gate per test

- A test **fails** if any of its axes scores -3 or lower.
- Otherwise the test **passes**. Name its lowest axis.
- A strong market passes all three tests.

Report the three test results and the weakest axis overall. Give no total
and no mean, whether across axes or across tests.

## Quality gate

Before you deliver, confirm:

- all nine axes appear, each with a bull case, a bear case, an integer
  score, a confidence level, and a one-sentence reason
- axis 1 shows the bottom-up arithmetic against $1bn, not $100M, and names
  the revenue shape
- a non-US market gets a US-first axis that names the US equivalent
- axis 4 names the wedge and the year-3 asset (crowded), or the reason the
  gap exists (untapped)
- axis 9 names the buyer and the user and estimates the learning cycles
- every factual claim has a link to a page that states it; everything else
  is labeled assumption or inference
- the rollup has a gate per test and no total or average
- the scores did not move toward what the user hoped for
