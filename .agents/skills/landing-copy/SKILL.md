# Landing Copy

A landing page has one job: convince a stranger in seconds that this product
gives them something they already want. Defaults fail at this. Left unspecified,
copy describes the product (feature lists, "Get Started", headlines only the team
understands) instead of selling the outcome.

## The Principle

People buy a desire, not a product. Every unstated decision reverts to describing
what you built rather than what the reader gets. The skill forces three concrete
commitments: the one desire (Phase 1), one screen that sells it (Phase 2), and a
catalog that catches the drift back to feature-talk (Phase 3).

One equation governs every choice below:

> **Conversion = Desire - (Labor + Confusion)**

Raise desire, cut the work the reader must do to act, and remove every "wait,
what is this?" moment. Most entries in the Conversion-Killer Catalog are just
labor or confusion in a copy costume. When a line isn't building desire, it had
better be removing one of the other two.

## When to Activate

- writing or rewriting a landing page, hero section, or section copy
- writing a headline, a CTA, or a product name
- positioning a product: deciding what it is and who it's for, in one line
- writing pricing-page copy
- auditing an existing page for conversion-killers

## Phase 1: Pin the position (before writing a word)

Vague positioning produces generic copy. Commit these in writing first. This is
the copy analog of `DESIGN.md`: an explicit brief you write against.

1. **One core function.** One sentence on the single job it does. Swiss Army
   knives aren't remembered. If you list three jobs, pick the one.
2. **The desire, not the feature.** Name the underlying want the function serves:
   money, time, health, status, or pain relief. Copy sells that, not the feature.
3. **Customer language.** Write the words real users use to describe the problem,
   stolen from reviews, support tickets, or interviews. Not internal jargon.
4. **The ten-word pitch.** The whole product in one sentence a stranger repeats.
5. **The name.** Known words, not invented jargon. A name people can say and
   spell after hearing it once.
6. **What's actually new.** Name the genuinely novel angle. Clones don't spread.
   If there's a live trend or conversation to connect to, name that too.
7. **The top objection.** Ask buyers (or churned trials) "What almost stopped
   you from buying?" The most common answer is the one fear the page has to
   disarm out loud. Mine it before writing, not after launch.

Output: a short positioning brief covering all seven. Write the page only from
it.

## Phase 2: Write the page

The page has a standard skeleton. Fill it top to bottom; each block does one job:

1. **Navbar** - logo, the few links that matter, pricing among them.
2. **Hero** - header, subheader, one image or demo, one CTA.
3. **Social proof** - customer or press logos, directly under the hero.
4. **CTA** - the primary action, stated as an outcome.
5. **Features and objections** - three to six blocks, each selling one value
   prop and disarming one objection.
6. **Repeat CTA** - the same action again, for readers who scrolled to decide.
7. **Footer** - the rest, plus one memorable line and a thumbnail-grade OG image.

Then make each block earn its place:

1. **The hero sells alone.** Assume most visitors never scroll. The first screen
   must convert by itself: what it is, the desire it serves, one action.
2. **Empathy before the pitch.** State the problem better than the reader could
   state it themselves, first. Earn the sale by proving you understand it. Then
   answer the top objection from Phase 1 head-on, in the feature block nearest
   the doubt, before the reader talks themselves out of it.
3. **Show, don't explain.** A demo, screenshot, or screen recording beats
   paragraphs. Put your best feature on the page, not behind the paywall.
4. **Headlines:** fifth-grade reading level (your mother understands it); trigger
   curiosity or emotion over feature-recitation; numbers over adjectives ("save 4
   hours a week", not "fast"). Draft five, keep the one still recalled a day later.
5. **One idea per screen.** Each section makes exactly one point.
6. **One CTA, named by outcome.** A single primary path. Label it with verb +
   outcome ("Analyze my website"), never "Get Started" or "Learn More". Multiple
   competing buttons create hesitation.
7. **Strong, picturable language.** Concrete claims the reader can visualize. Cut
   weak qualifiers ("can help you potentially").
8. **Copy a competitor can't paste.** Written from your specific experience and
   your customer's words. If it fits any rival's site, it's generic.
9. **Proof.** Testimonials collected before traffic arrives. A visible founder
   (face, voice, screen recording) beats corporate stock.
10. **Findable pricing and a strong footer.** Pricing is the first thing many
    visitors look for: put it in the nav. Most won't buy but may share, so end
    with something memorable. Design the OG image like a thumbnail.

## Phase 3: Business-model defaults (opinionated, name the assumption)

These are bets, not laws. They hold for an indie or micro-SaaS product sold
directly to its user. Before applying any, state the assumption out loud. They
are **wrong** for product-led-growth funnels, marketplaces, network-effect
products, and enterprise sales. Flag the mismatch rather than applying blindly.

- **Premium over cheap.** Don't compete on being the cheapest.
- **Three tiers, good/better/best.** Not a pricing matrix.
- **One-time over subscription** where the value isn't recurring.
- **Hard paywall / no free plan** to validate real demand before building more.

If the product is PLG, a marketplace, or enterprise, say so and set these aside.

## Conversion-Killer Catalog

The tell, then the antidote. When you catch one, rewrite.

| Tell | Antidote |
|------|----------|
| "Get Started" / "Sign Up" / "Learn More" | verb + outcome: "Analyze my website" |
| Two or more competing CTAs | one primary path; demote the rest |
| Adjectives: "fast", "powerful", "seamless" | a number or picturable outcome |
| Feature list as the hero | the desire the feature delivers |
| A paragraph explaining the product | show it: demo, screenshot, recording |
| Invented brand jargon as the name | known words people can say and spell |
| Headline only the team understands | fifth-grade reading level |
| Copy that fits any competitor | your experience, your customer's words |
| Buried or missing pricing | pricing in the nav |
| No proof on the page | testimonials, founder face |
| Hero that needs a scroll to make sense | self-contained first screen |
| The top objection left unanswered | name it and disarm it in a feature block |

## Quality Gate

Before delivering, confirm:

- the positioning brief exists and the page traces to it
- the hero converts with no scroll: what, desire, one action
- exactly one primary CTA, labeled by outcome
- the page leads with the problem before the pitch
- the top objection from Phase 1 is named and disarmed on the page
- claims use numbers or proof, not adjectives
- at least one testimonial and a visible founder
- no Conversion-Killer Catalog tell survives
- business-model defaults applied only after the assumption is stated, or set
  aside with the reason
- prose follows the `writing` skill's anti-slop rules (no em dashes, no buzzwords)
