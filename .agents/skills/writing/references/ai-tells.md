# AI tells

The full catalog of patterns that mark prose as machine-written. Merged from
this skill's original banned list, blader/humanizer
(github.com/blader/humanizer, itself built on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI
writing"), the slopdetector.org taxonomy, the how-to-ai.guide "You sound
like AI" list (adverb abuse, negative parallelism, the grand
pronouncement), and the cadence failures observed in this skill's own
drafts. SKILL.md carries the core
rules; load this file when de-slopping, auditing, or writing anything long
enough to drift.

## How to use this list

- **Cluster over isolation.** One tell is noise; a cluster is a confession.
  Rewrite when tells stack, not on a single match.
- **Rewrite, don't delete.** Replace the tell with a natural sentence that
  covers the same ground; cutting content to dodge a tell is its own tell.
- **Preserve human signals.** Mixed registers, genuine asides, unresolved
  tension, dated references, and specific hard-to-fabricate detail are what
  human writing looks like. Don't sand them off.
- **Leave quotes alone.** Quoted material, titles, and text under discussion
  are not yours to fix.

## Sentence templates

- "It's not about X, it's about Y" and other negative parallelisms
  ("not only… but…", tailing fragments like "no guessing")
- "No X. No Y. Just Z."
- "In today's fast-paced world" / "rapidly evolving landscape"
- "The key is…" as a standalone insight
- "Studies show…" / "experts argue" / "industry reports" with no named source
- "here's why this matters" and other empty bridges
- a closing question added only to juice engagement
- aphorism formulas: "X is the language of Y", "X becomes a trap"
- false ranges: "from X to Y" where X and Y form no real spectrum
- rule-of-three overuse: ideas forced into triples for fake comprehensiveness
- "The real question is…" / "at its core, what really matters" framing theater

## Cadence

The rhythm tells survive every word ban, because they are made of ordinary
words. They are the strongest current signal of machine prose.

- the punchline paragraph: a sentence of evidence, then a verdict of three
  to six words, then a paragraph break ("Everything got quieter. Nothing
  got resolved.")
- the two-beat contrast as a device: "X. Y." or "X, not Y" ("They aren't
  guessing. They're recalling." "It's the attractor, not the budget.")
- the grand pronouncement: "That is the finding worth building on", "This
  isn't a budget. It's a statement of intent.", "The honest names."
- one-word and two-word paragraphs ("Numbers.")
- colon-fronted announcements: "So:", "Side by side:", "The result:"
- rule-of-three fragment lists ("Tests that fail. Code that throws. A
  transaction that will not reconcile.")
- the epigram close: a last line that restates the thesis as a slogan
- adverb-verb pairs that fake precision: "quietly runs", "neatly sidesteps"
- missing glue: causal links left for the reader to infer from
  juxtaposition instead of stated with then, so, which, because, hence

## Vocabulary and grammar

- the AI word cluster: delve, tapestry, landscape, underscore, foster,
  crucial, pivotal, vibrant, interplay, showcase, boast, enhance, robust,
  seamless, realm, foundational
- copula avoidance: "serves as", "stands as", "marks", "features" where
  "is" belongs
- superficial "-ing" tails that fake analysis: "…, highlighting the
  importance of", "…, reflecting the community's connection"
- elegant variation: cycling synonyms for one concept ("the protagonist…
  the main character… the hero")
- filler: "in order to", "due to the fact that", "at this point in time"
- hedge stacks: "could potentially possibly", qualifiers protecting the
  writer instead of naming conditions
- hyphenated pairs in predicate position ("the report is high-quality")

## Texture and tone

- em dashes (hard constraint: zero in final output; en dashes too)
- promotional language: "boasts", "nestled", "breathtaking", "game-changer",
  "cutting-edge", "revolutionary"
- significance puffery: "stands as a testament", "pivotal", "marking a
  shift"
- manufactured staccato drama: strings of short fragments for inflated tone
- fake-candid openers: "Honestly?", "Look,", "Here's the thing"
- sycophancy: "Great question!", "You're absolutely right"
- generic positive conclusions: bright futures, exciting journeys ahead
- literary flourish, vague profundity, motivational language
- fake vulnerability arcs; biography padding that moves no argument

## Structure and format

- signposting: "Let's dive in", "without further ado", announcing instead
  of starting
- a heading followed by a one-line restatement before real content
- inline-header lists: "**User Experience:** the user experience has been…"
- mechanical boldface; emojis decorating headers; Title Case Headings
- formulaic "Challenges and Future Prospects" sections
- diff-anchored writing: narrating the change ("this was added to replace…")
  instead of describing what is
- collaborative artifacts: "I hope this helps", "Let me know if…"
- speculative gap-filling: "likely grew up…", "maintains a low profile" —
  say what isn't known or cut the sentence

## The five slop categories

From slopdetector.org/slop-taxonomy, with their tells:

1. **Generic** ("In today's fast-paced world…"): templated, no concrete detail.
2. **Pseudo-insight** ("The key is to find balance…"): sounds deep, says nothing.
3. **Fake authority** ("Studies have shown…"): authoritative tone, zero sources.
4. **Wikipedia rehash** ("X is defined as…"): definitions sold as analysis.
5. **Wellness** ("Self-care isn't selfish…"): universal advice that helps no one.

The antidote to all five is the same: specificity, lived experience (real,
from the brief), cited sources, genuine information gain.
