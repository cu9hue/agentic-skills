The Unix philosophy came out of Bell Labs between 1964 and 1978. It started as habits among the people who built Unix. Doug McIlroy, who ran the group, wrote those habits down afterwards.

**1964: the garden-hose memo.** Five years before Unix existed, McIlroy wrote an internal memo during the Multics project:

> "We should have some ways of connecting programs like garden hose--screw in another segment when it becomes when it becomes necessary to massage data in another way." ([transcribed memo](https://interfacelineage.com/malleable-interfaces); the repeated phrase is in the original)

He was drawing on coroutines (Conway, then Dahl's Simula) and thinking of programs as stages in a data stream ([McIlroy email, quoted by Hauben](https://www.columbia.edu/~hauben/book-pdf/CHAPTER%209.pdf)).

**1969: a reaction to Multics.** Bell Labs left Multics, a large and ambitious system. Ken Thompson started Unix on a cast-off PDP-7, and Dennis Ritchie soon joined him. The goal was modest: an environment where they could comfortably do their own programming work. Small size was a constraint, and then it became a value.

**1972: pipes.** McIlroy kept pushing his idea. Ritchie later called the first refusal "a failure of imagination". Thompson added pipes in 1972, "at the suggestion (or perhaps insistence) of M. D. McIlroy" ([Ritchie, via Hauben](https://cscie26.dce.harvard.edu/~dce-lib113/reference/unix/unix2.html)). The `|` notation made composition cheap, people coined the word "filter", and many old commands were changed to work in pipelines. This is the point where the style really took hold. The philosophy is mostly a description of what pipes reward.

**1978: the maxims get written down.** McIlroy, E. N. Pinson and B. A. Tague wrote the foreword to the Unix issue of the *Bell System Technical Journal*. They framed the rules as maxims that "have gained currency among the builders and users" ([BSTJ, July–August 1978](https://vtda.org/pubs/BSTJ/vol57-1978/articles/bstj57-6-1899.pdf)):

1. "Make each program do one thing well. To do a new job, build afresh rather than complicate old programs by adding new 'features.'"
2. "Expect the output of every program to become the input to another, as yet unknown, program."
3. Build software to be tried early, "ideally within weeks", and throw away the clumsy parts.
4. Use tools rather than manual labor, even if you must stop to build the tools first.

The same foreword cites Schumacher's "Small is beautiful".

**Later summaries made it famous.** Peter Salus's *A Quarter Century of Unix* (1994) gave McIlroy's three-line version, the one most people quote: write programs that do one thing and do it well, write programs that work together, and use text streams, because text is a universal interface. Kernighan and Pike's *The UNIX Programming Environment* (1984) taught the style to a generation. Eric Raymond's *The Art of Unix Programming* (2003) expanded it into 17 named rules.

The short version: the philosophy wasn't designed up front. It came from a small team on small machines, plus McIlroy's long-held idea about connecting programs. Once pipes made composition easy, small single-purpose tools became the obvious way to work, and the rules described that afterwards.
